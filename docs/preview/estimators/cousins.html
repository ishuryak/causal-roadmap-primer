Partialling out frailty · Frisch–Waugh–Lovell

Regress schedule on frailty and keep the residuals. Do the same for mortality. Then regress the mortality residuals on the schedule residuals. Frisch–Waugh–Lovell tells us that this slope equals the schedule coefficient from the full least-squares regression, using the same rows and adjustment variables.

Partially linear DML uses predictions from the other folds to remove the part of schedule and mortality predicted by frailty. Collect the two residuals for every held-out record, then fit one slope across all 5,000 pairs.

Sum of residual products ÷ sum of squared schedule residuals

−43.65 ÷ 1,005 ≈ is approximately −0.04343

That slope is −4.34 percentage points. It matches ordinary adjusted regression in this example. With a binary schedule, the partial slope weights each frailty group by its size times e(1 − e), where e is its probability of short. Lower-frailty patients make up 60% of Susan’s cohort and get 62.69% of the weight in the slope. Their treatment effect differs from the higher-frailty group’s, so the change in weights changes the answer. Read the FWL result.

The fitting records and sources

Both methods use the same 5,000 synthetic records. There are 3,000 lower-frailty and 2,000 higher-frailty patients. In the DML example, folds are balanced by frailty and schedule. Treatment probabilities and risks are re-estimated within each training set.

A causal interpretation requires comparable groups given frailty, a chance of either schedule in each group, well-defined schedules and complete follow-up here. See the counts and assumptions.
