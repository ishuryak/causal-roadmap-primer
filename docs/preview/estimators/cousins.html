TMLE and DML

How would five-year mortality change across Susan’s 5,000 eligible patients under short versus standard radiotherapy? Targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE) and double machine learning (DML) both use risk and treatment models to answer her question.

Method

TMLE updates the predicted risk under each schedule, then compares the two averages.

Partialling out frailty · Frisch–Waugh–Lovell

First regress schedule on frailty and keep the residual. Do the same for mortality. The Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem says the slope between these residuals equals the schedule coefficient in the full least-squares regression, using the same rows and adjustment columns.

Partially linear DML (PLR) extends this idea using predictions from other folds. The patient’s two residuals above use the model trained without fold 1. The slope uses all 5,000 held-out pairs. In this example it matches the ordinary adjusted regression.

Look back at the Target and Correct steps. Both use the gap between observed and predicted mortality. TMLE uses that gap to update the risks. DML for the average effect uses it to correct each patient’s contribution.

Here the schedule is binary. PLR gives each frailty group weight proportional to its size times e(1 − e), where e is the probability of short. Lower-frailty patients get 62.69% of the weight in that slope and 60% in Susan’s all-eligible average. The two group effects differ, so the answers differ too. Read the FWL result.

The fitting records and sources

TMLE fits the initial risks and treatment probabilities in the 5,000-record cohort. For DML, each of five folds holds out 1,000 records while the other 4,000 supply the predictions. The six display patients belong to no fold. Their DML illustrations use the model trained without fold 1.

For a causal interpretation, Susan needs comparable groups given frailty, a real chance of either schedule within each group, well-defined schedules and complete follow-up in this example. See the counts and assumptions.
