Another DML target · partialling out frailty

Partially linear regression (PLR). Predict schedule and mortality separately from frailty, training on the other folds. Subtract each prediction from what happened. Fit one slope relating the two sets of held-out residuals.

V = observed schedule − predicted chance of short. U = observed death indicator − predicted mortality given frailty alone. The ATE calculation above uses a different outcome residual, from the risk under the schedule actually received.

Slope = sum of V × U divided by sum of V²

−43.65 ÷ 1,005 ≈ is approximately −0.04343

That gives −4.34 percentage points. Lower frailty has a +2-point schedule difference and higher frailty a −15-point difference. The ATE counts patients, giving those groups 60% and 40% of the weight. The partial slope weights each group by its size times e(1 − e), where e is its chance of short. Its weights here are 62.69% and 37.31%.

The Frisch–Waugh–Lovell (FWL) connection. In ordinary least squares, removing the same adjustment variables from schedule and outcome on the same records gives exactly the schedule coefficient from the full regression. Cross-fitting uses different training records. The two slopes happen to agree here because every fold has the same treatment shares within frailty groups. Read the FWL result.

The fitting records and sources

Both methods use the same 5,000 synthetic records. There are 3,000 lower-frailty and 2,000 higher-frailty patients. In the DML example, folds are balanced by frailty and schedule. Treatment probabilities and risks are re-estimated within each training set.

A causal interpretation requires comparable groups given frailty, a chance of either schedule in each group, well-defined schedules and complete follow-up here. See the counts and assumptions.
