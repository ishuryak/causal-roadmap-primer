Estimator field guide

Susan wants to compare five-year mortality under short and standard radiation schedules. How does each method use her records?

Outcome regressionS- and T-learners
S fits one outcome model with schedule as an input. T fits a separate model per schedule. Both predict two risks per baseline profile. Averaging each risk over the same patients and subtracting standard from short gives the g-computation estimate.
Effect regressionX- and R-learners
X pairs each observed outcome with the fitted risk under the other schedule to form a signed pseudo-outcome. It fits these contrasts by arm and blends the predictions. R removes predictable variation in treatment and outcome, then uses the residuals to estimate the effect at each baseline profile.
WeightingIPW
For the average treatment effect, inverse probability weighting (IPW) weights each observed outcome by the inverse probability of the schedule received. The propensity score is the probability of short given baseline characteristics.
Combined methodsAIPW and TMLE
Augmented IPW (AIPW) corrects predicted risks using inverse-probability-weighted residuals. Targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE) uses treatment probabilities to update the predicted risks, then averages the updated risks under each schedule.
Which patients? CATE, ATE and ATT

The estimand states the contrast and population. An estimator is the rule for calculating it. The resulting number is the estimate. Here, short minus standard is a five-year mortality risk difference. A negative difference means lower mortality under short.

CATE
The conditional average treatment effect compares the two risks at the same baseline profile, such as higher frailty.
ATE
The average treatment effect gives each of the 5,000 eligible patients equal weight.
ATT
The average treatment effect among the treated uses the baseline mix of the 2,600 patients who received short.
Base learners, Super Learner and cross-fitting

A base learner is a prediction algorithm, such as logistic regression or a forest. A meta-learner arranges prediction fits to estimate treatment effects. Super Learner uses cross-validation to combine candidate algorithms for a prediction task, such as fitting mortality risk inside a T-learner.

Outcome risks and treatment probabilities are nuisance functions used to estimate the causal contrast. Cross-fitting trains such functions on other folds before predicting for a held-out fold. Double machine learning (DML) commonly pairs cross-fitting with an orthogonal score that reduces first-order sensitivity to small errors in those fits.

For a causal interpretation, each schedule must be well defined and a patient’s observed outcome must agree with their outcome under the schedule received (consistency). We assume one patient’s schedule does not affect another patient’s outcome, frailty captures the common causes of treatment and mortality (conditional exchangeability), and either schedule is possible in each target group (positivity). The exercise assumes complete five-year follow-up.
