What changes the answer?

One regression or two?

In S, the same fitted outcome model predicts under both schedules. In T, each schedule gets a separate fit. An ordinary logistic S model with separate intercepts and frailty slopes has exactly the same fitted risks as two ordinary logistic regressions using the same frailty predictor.

Turn off the interaction in S. A shared frailty slope also forces a common treatment odds ratio. The fitted risk difference can still vary with frailty. In the lower-frailty group, even its sign changes in this example.

Why does X blend two estimates?

Each second-stage fit estimates the same conditional contrast. When short schedules are uncommon, the larger standard arm can help estimate the missing standard risk for short recipients. One choice gives weight e to the fit from standard recipients and weight 1 − e to the fit from short recipients, where e is the chance of receiving short at that profile.

Here the two second-stage estimates coincide within each frailty group. In a separate arithmetic example, suppose they were −2 and −6 points and e were 0.25. Their weighted estimate would be 0.25 × (−2) + 0.75 × (−6) = −5 points. The choice of weights and smoothing can matter in other datasets.

How does this fit the trial evidence?

Random allocation makes treatment groups comparable in expectation at assignment within a trial. A resident can still ask how well a trial’s result applies to patients seen less often in it. Susan would first check the indication, schedules, age, frailty and follow-up against the trial. Her records can extend that inquiry where the design supports a fair comparison.

Real follow-up can end before five years. Studying survival through time, or average months lived, also requires appropriate survival and censoring methods. Return to Susan’s clinical decision.

Field guide to the vocabulary

What is the target and what estimates it?
Estimand
The quantity a study aims to learn. Here it is a five-year mortality risk difference for a specified population.
Estimator and estimate
An estimator is the rule applied to data. The estimate is the number it returns.
CATE, ATE and ATT
A conditional average treatment effect (CATE) compares mean outcomes for patients with a specified baseline profile. The average treatment effect (ATE) averages over the eligible population. The average treatment effect among the treated (ATT) averages over people who received the shorter schedule here.
Base learner and meta-learner
A base learner fits a regression or prediction function, such as a logistic regression or a forest. A meta-learner arranges one or more such fits to estimate a causal contrast under identification assumptions.
Nuisance functions and Super Learner
Nuisance functions are quantities estimated along the way to the treatment effect, such as baseline-specific outcome risks or treatment probabilities. Super Learner is a prediction ensemble that uses cross-validation to combine candidate algorithms for a fit like this.
Which part of the calculation does a method change?
Outcome regression and g-computation
Predict outcomes under each schedule for the same baseline profiles, then standardize by averaging those predictions over a chosen population. S shares a fit across schedules. T fits each arm separately.
Learning an effect function
X forms signed pseudo-outcomes from the observed outcome and the prediction under the other schedule. Its second-stage fits learn how those contrasts vary. The R-learner instead relates residual outcome variation to residual treatment variation after accounting for baseline covariates.
Propensity and weighting
The propensity score is the probability of receiving short conditional on baseline covariates. Inverse probability weighting (IPW) uses treatment probabilities to reweight observed outcomes for a target contrast. X can use propensity scores to blend its two effect fits.
Augmentation and targeting
Augmented IPW (AIPW) combines outcome predictions with weighted outcome residuals. Targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE) updates initial predictions using treatment probabilities and the target parameter, then summarizes the updated predictions.
Cross-arm imputation, cross-fitting and overlap
Cross-arm imputation
A patient’s predicted risk under the other schedule supplies one term of X’s pseudo-outcome. The patient’s unobserved binary outcome stays unknown.
Cross-fitting
Split the fitting sample into folds. Fit a model on other folds and predict for the held-out fold, repeating until each record has an out-of-fold prediction. This can accompany several estimators.
Double machine learning (DML)
DML uses an orthogonal score, an estimating rule designed to be insensitive to small first-order errors in the nuisance fits. It commonly uses cross-fitting to supply those predictions.
Overlap and positivity
At each relevant baseline profile, both schedules must be possible to identify this contrast from the records. Sparse observed arms can make estimates unstable. A structural zero removes the within-profile comparison.
