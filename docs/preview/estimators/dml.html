Partialling out frailty

What does “adjust for frailty” do in a regression?

Start with ordinary least squares. Within each frailty group, subtract the group’s short-course proportion from each patient’s schedule and its mortality rate from each patient’s outcome. Fit a line through the two sets of residuals. Its slope equals the schedule coefficient in a regression of mortality on schedule and frailty. This is the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell (FWL) theorem.

The partially linear DML estimate

Now fit the schedule and mortality models on the other folds. For every held-out patient, calculate V = schedule − predicted chance of short and U = outcome − predicted mortality given frailty alone. Pool the residuals and fit the slope.

Slope = sum of V × U divided by sum of V²

−43.65 ÷ 1,005 ≈ is approximately −0.04343

The partial slope is −4.34 percentage points. It matches the full-data line here because every fold has the same treatment shares within frailty groups. The exact FWL identity applies to ordinary least squares fitted on the same records.

Why does the cohort estimate above give −4.80 points? The lower- and higher-frailty differences are +2 and −15 points. The cohort average weights them by patient counts (60% and 40%). The partial slope weights each group by its size times e(1 − e), where e is its chance of short. That gives 62.69% and 37.31%. In a trial with the same randomization probability in both groups, the two population weightings coincide.
